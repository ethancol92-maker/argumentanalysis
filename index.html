    Text type

    Work out the form of a text from its features, and think about why the form matters.

    Progressive or conservative values

    Decide which readers a belief or a line of text is most likely to appeal to.

    What do these labels mean?

    Progressive values

    A general preference for social change and reform, with a larger role for government in solving problems.

    Often linked with social justice, equality, public services and protecting the environment.

    Conservative values

    A general preference for tradition and stability, with a smaller role for government.

    Often linked with individual responsibility, private enterprise, lower taxes and careful spending.

    These are broad labels. Most people hold a mix of views, and some texts are written for everyone. The labels are useful because they help you explain why certain words would work on a certain audience.

    Tone in vocabulary choices

    Tone is the writer’s attitude, and it shows in the words they choose. Name the tone, then find the words that create it.

    Introduction: CDFASTCATP

    Cover these ten details in three or four sentences.

      Each body paragraph: What, How, Why, So

      Open with What. Then repeat How, Why and So as many times as you need.

      What

      What is the writer arguing at this point, and in what tone?

      Kennett opens in an alarmed tone, arguing that Australia relies too heavily on imports.

      How

      Which words or image does the writer use? Quote them.

      He calls Australia “a begging country”.

      Why

      How do these words support the argument?

      “Begging” suggests weakness and shame, which frames reliance on imports as a national failure.

      So

      What is the audience meant to think, feel or do?

      Herald Sun readers who value self-reliance are positioned to feel embarrassed and to want local production restored.

      Three body paragraphs, no separate conclusion

      ParagraphCovers
      1How the text opens, the first main argument and the tone.
      2The second argument, any change in tone, and how the piece develops.
      3The final argument, any change in tone or structure, and the call to action.

      Analyse the image beside the argument it supports, or where it sits in the text.

      What assessors reward

      • Using the background information to work out the audience and the writer’s purpose.
      • Explaining why the writer puts ideas in this order.
      • Choosing the most persuasive parts instead of covering everything.
      • Explaining how words target what this audience values.
      • Analysing the image as closely as the words.
      • Showing how argument, language and image work together.

      What holds responses back

      • Summarising what the writer says.
      • Naming a technique without explaining its effect.
      • Comments that could fit any text: “inclusive language makes the reader feel included”.
      • Overstating: “this forces the reader to agree”.
      • Stating the obvious: “uses comparison to compare”.
      • Only describing the image, or leaving it out.
